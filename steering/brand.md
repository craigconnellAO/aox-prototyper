# AO Brand Guidelines

> Source: AO Brand Guidelines site (brand.ao.com) — Design and Copy sections

## The AO look and feel

To make sure AO's "always on" personality is clear to see, the brand relies on colour, graphics, icons, typography, and voice working together. Each element below is designed for flexibility while staying unmistakably AO.

---

# 1. Logo

Our logo is a friendly smile with an "a" and an "o" for eyes — everything great about AO in one nifty design. It works at all sizes, digital and physical.

## Logo in colour

The logo is primarily green on a white background, or white on green (reversed). It can also sit on any colour from the full palette where that works better. Go greener for audiences that know AO least, and always be aware of correct logo placement/contrast. The logo has been shown working in white, green, light green, blue, black, navy, peach, dark green, and coral variants against complementary background colours.

## Logo on imagery

When placing the logo on photography or graphics, always make sure it has enough contrast and isn't positioned on a busy part of the image. Use the white version of the logo on darker/plainer parts of a photo; use the dark version on lighter or patterned backgrounds.

## Clear space

Leave a minimum clear space around the logo equal to the size of the "o" from the logo at any given size. This isolates it from text and supporting graphics. When in doubt, give it more room rather than less.

## Minimum sizes

Print: minimum width of 5mm. Digital: can scale down to 32px in width and still look good. The favicon is an exception to this rule.

## Optical alignment

The logo is built to fit in a square, but the rounded smile creates more visual negative space below than above. To optically centre it: scale the logo to 55% of the width of your square/circle, align the smile at this size to the top of the shape, then align the top of the logo to the bottom of that reference smile.

## Lock-up with URL

When locking up the URL with the logo: the space between the bottom of the logo and the x-height of the type equals half the "o" from the logo (A); the x-height of the type equals half the "o" from the logo (B); always use "Smiley Face Bold" with a kerning of -15, and the type colour should always match the logo colour (C). The URL should always be written as "ao.extension" — never with "www." and never with the extension used alone (e.g. never just ".com" or ".de").

## Logo: things to avoid

Do not use the logo in black on coloured visuals. Do not crop the logo. Do not distort or rotate the logo. Do not use the logo in an outlined version. Do not have multiple logos in the same layout. Do not use the logo in colours that don't contrast with the background. Do not apply colour gradients or effects to the logo. Do not add the URL to the horizontal lockup. Do not place the logo on busy parts of an image.

## Colour: things to avoid

Avoid making the logo or other "foreground" elements the same colour as the graphics behind them. Avoid colour combinations with low contrast. Avoid assigning different colours to elements on the same layer. Avoid combinations that look sickly — use best judgement. Avoid introducing new colours/tones or using gradients. Avoid assigning full-palette colours exclusively to specific UI categories or pages (e.g. always using one colour only for "Deals" or "Finance").

# 3. 2D Graphic Language

## Our elements

Patterns, supergraphics, and category graphics all use graphic shapes derived from the smile logo: the "o", the counter (dot) of the "o", the smile arc, and smaller sections of the smile. The "a" is never used as a graphic element since it's too recognisable as a letter.

## Our smile

The smile can be sectioned to create additional expressions used across the graphic language: **Smirk** (a small corner section), **Grin** (a mid-size section), and **Smile** (the full arc).

## Patterns

Patterns come in three variations, from the more complex "AO pattern" down to a simple "smirk pattern," for when the brand wants to be expressive. Confirmed variants include a Smile pattern and a Smirk pattern (repeating curved line motifs). Used across social media, email, packaging, umbrellas, taxi wraps, and office wall murals.

## Supergraphics

Supergraphics are amplified elements of the logo that create bold, abstract forms — used to highlight products, accompany headlines, or frame photography. Four core supergraphic shapes: **Smirk**, **Dot**, **Circle**, **Smile**. Seen in use behind headlines (e.g. "Good vibes delivered."), on apology/service pages, and framing product imagery.

## Category graphics

Distinct playful graphic patterns distinguish each product category, with the form of each pattern inspired by the function of the product it represents (e.g. kettles get a pattern that looks like bubbles of steam). Confirmed categories and their motifs: Heating & cooling (arcs), Kettles (bubble dots), Floorcare (sweeping leaf-like curves), Ovens & cooking (radiating segments), Speakers & TVs (concentric sound-wave arcs), Laundry (varied dots/spin), Smart Tech (scattered dots), Fridges & freezers (spiral swirl arcs). Used for category selector screens, social posts, and product category cards/headlines (e.g. "Ovens your chips would be proud of.").

# 4. Icons

## Our icons

Every AO icon hides a smile in the form of a subtle curve — making them unique to AO and "full of beans." Icons use a simple line style and appear across the palette's colours (e.g. green, orange/toast, coral/heat, blue/ice).

## Our icons are responsive

Icons are optimised for both digital and physical use. The larger they get, the more detailed they become: smaller icons stay minimal and functional, while icons larger than 128px become more elaborate. Scale reference: 16px, 24px, 32px, 48px (minimal) through to 128px+ (elaborate).

## Examples in use

Icons appear as small functional UI elements (navigation bars, delivery/finance/promotion tags on product pages) and as large elaborate illustrations (e.g. a broom-and-bucket icon used as a big physical/digital hero graphic).

# 5. Typography

## Our brand typeface

The brand typeface is **Smiley Face**, featuring characterful curves and forms inspired by the signature smile.

## Smiley Face Headline

The most expressive cut of the typeface — the Headline weight — used for headlines or to draw attention to large type. It features beautiful ligatures (seen in examples like "Washing machines," "Always On!," and "Bing!") that dial up brand personality. Because it's so expressive, Headline type can stand alone as a piece of creative without needing photography or graphic elements.

## Body weights

Smiley Face comes in four weights — **Bold, Medium, Regular, Light** — each with an italic version, giving flexibility and consistent type hierarchy across print and digital. Headline and Bold share the same weight, but Bold lacks the expressive ligatures, so Bold is used mostly for smaller functional headers rather than large expressive headlines.

## Typography: things to avoid

Do not rotate or distort the type in any way. Always use the headline type for large type — not any other weight. Do not use an outline on the type. Follow the brand's hierarchy rules when setting type. Make sure kerning and leading aren't overly tight or loose. Do not justify the type — instead centre-align or left-align it depending on the layout.

# 6. Tone of Voice

## Overview

AO's tone of voice is how the brand sounds — translating the "always on" personality into words for merch, social posts, printed banners, and more. Three principles work together, with a hint of all three present in every piece of copy, though each carries extra weight in different contexts:

- **Always happy to see you**
- **Always full of beans**
- **Always ready to help**

## Principle 1: Always happy to see you

**Traits:** Warm, Chatty, Positive

AO deals with people every day of the week and wouldn't have it any other way — friendly, real, and easy to understand. Copy should sound like someone you'd want to have a natter with ("grandma-friendly").

**Where it matters most:** key messaging on the website.

**Examples in practice:** personalised, warm greetings in account/navigation areas (e.g. "Welcome back Alison!"); chatty, self-aware brand introductions describing the team as "drivers, installation engineers, and all round agony aunts too"; friendly framing of delivery/installation services (e.g. pairing "Ordered online" with "Powered up in person," and noting the team personally delivers and installs products).

## Principle 2: Always full of beans

**Traits:** Warm, Chatty, Positive *(as labelled on the source page — this repeats the same trait set as Principle 1, which may be worth confirming with the brand team in case a distinct set was intended)*

It takes more than the weather to get AO down — every product is a thing of beauty, and the brand speaks passionately about everything it does, from stock updates to "epic deliveries against all odds." AO is a constant source of fun.

**Where it matters most:** social media and promotional comms.

**Examples in practice:** playful, celebratory social captions praising the team by name (e.g. "powered through storm Jorge to deliver and install [a customer's] first new oven in 20 years. Good man Johnny!"); short punchy reaction captions with emoji (e.g. "the finish on that 👏"); energetic promo banners for new product drops with casual calls to action (e.g. "Ooh. The new [product]'s here" + "Let's go!").

## Principle 3: Always ready to help

**Traits:** Empathetic, Practical, To the point

AO cares about customers' kitchen disasters and electrical nightmares because the team has "all been there" — empathetic and understanding, giving people the info they need without messing about.

**Where it matters most:** customer service.

**Examples in practice:** clear, reassuring FAQ/support copy inviting direct contact (e.g. "If there's something wrong with your order, we want to know about it! Call us on [number]..."); confident, personality-led service reassurance (e.g. "Bish bash bosh? Never. Our install team are speedy, but it's never a rush-job."); sincere, human service-recovery copy (e.g. "A big apology from us — Hey [Name]! We're sorry to hear your delivery didn't happen. We'll look into it straight away.").

## Copy: things to avoid ("Let's go")

Don't use "Let's go" in a lockup with the logo. "Let's go" is sometimes featured in comms but is **not** a slogan — don't sign everything off with it. Don't capitalise "Go" — always write it in sentence case as "Let's go," never "Let's Go."

## Spelling AO

"AO" is always capitalised when spelling the name in a sentence. When part of a URL, it's always lowercase (e.g. ao.com). Colleagues are referred to as "AOers" — no apostrophe between the "O" and the "e."

## Diversity and Inclusion

AO is proud to be different, and takes Diversity and Inclusion seriously because being different is the brand's "superpower" — it makes the team stronger, more creative, and better at what they do. Communication should be fair, respectful, and use inclusive language that recognises all backgrounds, races, ethnicities, genders, ages, religions, abilities, and sexual orientations.

Top tips for inclusive language:

1. **Put people first** — recognise the human before the descriptor (e.g. "person of colour," "person who is blind," "person who is deaf").
2. **Empower, don't limit** — talk about what people can do, not what they can't (e.g. "person who uses a wheelchair").
3. **Perfect your pronouns** — prefer natural terms (e.g. "woman" over "female"); use whichever personal pronouns someone is comfortable with (including "they/them"), and ask respectfully if in doubt. Avoid gendered greetings like "Hey guys" for mixed groups — use "Hi folks," "Hello team," or "Morning everyone" instead.
4. **Don't lean on stereotypes** — it's fine to depict everyday activities, but never imply an activity or trait belongs to only one gender, or that happiness/wellbeing depends on a "beach ready" body or "Insta worthy" look. AO doesn't buy into "just for men" or "not for girls" framing, and the playful tone should never be used to mock someone for not conforming to gender stereotypes.
5. **Separate ethnicity from religion** — e.g. someone from Egypt, Iraq, or Morocco may be described as Arab (a geographic descriptor) and may or may not follow the Muslim faith.
6. **Respect age** — use "older person" or "young person." Reference points: 12 and under = child; 12–18 = teenager/young person.
7. **Be specific about sexuality** — avoid broad terms like "homosexual"; use "gay," "lesbian," or "bisexual" instead.

**Toolkit:** Microsoft Word's inclusive language checker (via Proofing settings) flags gender-specific language, ethnic slurs, mental health bias, and clichés/jargon. The **Gender Decoder** website can also be used to paste in copy and check it for gendered language.
